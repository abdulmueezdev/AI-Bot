Kafka Reads Marcus Aurelius

From the Diaries of Franz Kafka (Prague, Late November 1922)

*

I. The Emperor and the Clerk: An Unlikely Communion

Lying here in the semi-darkness of my room—where the ticking of the wall-clock seems to carve away pieces of my flesh with every stroke, and my lungs rattle like dry leaves in an autumn gale—I have spent these past weeks in the company of a ghost. Max, in his relentless desire to save my soul (or perhaps merely to keep my mind from devouring itself), brought me a translation of the Meditations of the Emperor Marcus Aurelius Antoninus [15, 27]. A strange, heavy book [27]. A notebook written in army tents along the frozen Danube, at Carnuntum, while the empire crumbled and the plague swept through the ranks [2, 196, 274].

How absurd it is. He was the master of the known world, an absolute sovereign who could, with a single nod, erase cities or condemn legions [196, 274]; I was a minor clerk, a temporarily retired Obersekretär of the Workers’ Accident Insurance Institute of Prague, whose greatest administrative triumph was reclassifying the risk factor of wood-planing machines or drafting a psychiatric flyer for mutilated war veterans [91, 100, 103, 108]. Yet, as I read his words by the failing light of a single candle, I feel a terrifying, blood-relation closeness to this Roman [129, 274]. He did not write a philosophy for the lecture halls; he wrote a diary to himself, in the dead of night, to remind himself of how to think and act [196]. 

Like me, he was a man trapped in an office he did not want, forced to perform duties that felt hollow, surrounded by a crowd of "envious, jealous, malignant people" from whom he could not escape [196, 274]. He was a clerk of the cosmos, laboring under the weight of an infinite, crushing administration [274]. He wrote to reconcile himself to a fate that was too large for his fragile frame [274]. But where he found a grand, harmonious court governed by divine reason, I see only the infinite corridors of an attic law court, where the judges are corrupt, the books are filled with dirt, and the accused is executed like a dog [74, 88].

*

II. The Resonances: The Inward Court and the Puppet Strings

Several of the Emperor's self-reproofs resonate so deeply within me that they feel less like ink on paper and more like the needle of the torture machine in the penal colony, writing its silent verdict directly onto my skin [6]. 

Guilt and the Inward Accuser
Marcus Aurelius is obsessed with the "ruling part"—the hegemonikon [232, 278]—and his entire journal is a record of constant self-scrutiny, an endless tribunal where he plays the part of both the accuser and the accused. This is the very engine of my own existence. For me, guilt is not a moral failing that can be washed away by repentance; it is a physical, bureaucratic debt. The Emperor writes: "Do wrong to thyself, do wrong to thyself, my soul; but thou wilt no longer have the opportunity of honoring thyself. Every man’s life is sufficient. But thine is nearly finished, though thy soul reverences not itself, but places thy felicity in the souls of others." [340]

To live in constant awareness of one's own insufficiency, to feel that every gesture, every letter written or left unwritten, is an unpaid debt to some incomprehensible authority—this is the Stoic's daily exercise, and it is my lifelong disease [66, 68]. The "ruling part" of the Stoic is nothing other than the inward judge of my The Trial, an invisible court that sits in permanent session in the dark attics of our minds [74, 88]. We do not need a policeman on the street to arrest us; we carry our own executioners within our chest [117, 160].

The Bureaucracy of the Cosmos
What the Stoic calls "Universal Nature"—the grand, rational system where every leaf, every bee, and every human being has a designated, unalterable place [250, 360]—feels to me like the ultimate, terrifying parody of a state bureaucracy. In the Emperor’s eyes, this system is beautiful because it is orderly [366]. But to the clerk who has spent his life sorting through the appeals of mutilated quarry workers, this perfect order is a trap [103, 141]. 

If everything is already "apportioned and spun out to thee" from the beginning of eternity [289, 354], then the individual is reduced to a mere case file in an infinite archive. There is no appeal, no avenue for recourse. The "Universal Nature" is a faceless, unapproachable department head. We are told the system is just, but the rules are kept hidden from us [74]. We sit before the gate of the Law, waiting for an entry that has been pre-ordained yet is eternally delayed [164].

The Puppet Strings and the Absurd
The Emperor repeatedly uses the image of the human being as a puppet: "No longer let this be a slave, no longer be pulled by the strings like a puppet to unsocial movements." [278, 338] He believed that by cultivating the "ruling part," one could cut these strings and stand free [232]. But he underestimates the horror of the puppet-state [375]. 

When I look at Gregor Samsa, or Georg Bendemann, or myself when I prepare to go to the office, I do not see a rational animal capable of cutting his strings; I see a creature whose very muscles, nerves, and thoughts are wired directly into the social machine [95, 96]. The tragedy is not that we are pulled by strings; the tragedy is that when the strings are finally cut, we do not stand upright—we collapse into a heap of useless rags, or we find that we have transformed into a monstrous vermin, capable only of waving our little legs helplessly in the air [23, 155].

*

III. The Rejection: The Cruelty of Harmony and the Illusion of Control

While I weep with the Emperor in his isolation, I must reject his solutions. His philosophy is a magnificent, gold-plated temple built over an open grave. He demands of himself—and of us—an optimism that is not merely impossible, but actively cruel.

The Trap of Rational Optimism
Marcus Aurelius insists that "nothing is evil which is according to nature," [274, 282] and that "the whole contains nothing which is not for its advantage." [403] How easily these words flow from the pen of an emperor! But let him say this to the stoker in the belly of the transatlantic ship [97], or to the young boy whose father has just condemned him to death by drowning [96, 154]. 

To claim that the suffering of the individual is justified because it serves the "advantage of the whole" is the ultimate language of administrative coldness [101, 106]. It is the justification of the Insurance Institute: a worker loses his fingers in a circular saw, and we calculate his compensation down to the last penny, reassuring him that his mutilation is merely a mathematically controlled distribution of risk within the industrial sector [101, 103]. The system remains balanced, the whole is preserved, but the man is ruined. I cannot accept a cosmic harmony that requires the slow, agonizing crushing of Gregor Samsa under the weight of an apple rotting in his back [70, 73].

The Illusion of the "Dichotomy of Control"
The very cornerstone of Stoicism—the distinction between what is "up to us" (our opinions, our desires, our inner will) and what is "not up to us" (our bodies, our reputations, our worldly success) [197, 205]—is a rationalist trap. The Stoic claims that if we simply withdraw our opinions, we are safe: "Cast away opinion: thou art saved." [418] 

But our inner world is not a secure, walled garden that can be shut off from the filth of the street. The mind and the body are fused in a terrifying, sadomasochistic embrace [153]. When my tuberculosis flares, when the blood rises in my throat, I cannot simply "wipe out my imagination" [316, 392] and declare that my lungs are "indifferent" [277, 302]. The illness is not an external "impression" that my mind can calmly evaluate; it is an active participant in my writing [108]. My lungs and my brain have made an agreement without my knowledge [111]. Gregor Samsa's transformation was not a matter of "opinion"; his insect body was a physical reality that dictated his every thought, his squeaking voice, his desire to crawl on the ceiling, his sudden, ravenous hunger for decaying garbage [46, 49, 50]. Stoicism demands that we remain "inviolate" within our minds [274, 282], but I know that the world writes its laws directly onto our bodies [85].

The Cruelty of "Amor Fati"
The Emperor commands us to love our fate, to accept whatever happens as a "grain of frankincense on the same altar." [288, 352] He asks us to look at the world from a "view from above," zooming out until our personal misery becomes as small as a drop of water in the ocean [198, 401]. 

But this zooming out does not bring peace; it only reveals the terrifying, cold indifference of the cosmos [53]. To see oneself as an invisible speck in an infinite void is not a comfort; it is the very definition of existential dread [8, 84]. I do not want to love my fate; my fate is a terminal diagnosis, a lifetime of sleeplessness, a mountain of unfinished manuscripts, and a father who looms over my life like a robust, worldly tyrant [28, 67, 108]. The Stoic path of absolute resignation leads to a quiet, self-sacrificing deterioration—an absolute submission that parodies freedom [20]. I would rather crawl into my burrow, like the mole-like creature of my stories, and perfect my underground tunnels in a state of exquisite, watchful paranoia, than pretend that the beast waiting outside does not exist [97, 145].

IV. The Emperor’s Verdict: Marcus Aurelius Reads The Trial and The Metamorphosis

If the Emperor were to sit in judgment of my writings, what would his verdict be? He would undoubtedly read them as case studies in philosophical failure, manuals of how a soul destroys itself through a lack of Stoic discipline.

On The Trial
Marcus Aurelius would look at Josef K.’s arrest and immediately point to a failure of "presence of mind" at the moment of awakening. He would say: "The moment of awakening is the riskiest moment in the day; once we’ve passed it without having been dragged away from our place, we can be calm." [149] Josef K. failed because, upon waking on his thirtieth birthday, he allowed himself to be distracted by Frau Grubach’s opinions, by his desire for breakfast, and by his immediate, defensive outrage [149, 150]. 

The Emperor would write in his notebook: "This clerk, Josef K., placed his happiness in the souls of other men [340]. He was terrified of what his bank colleagues would say, of how his reputation would suffer [150]. He did not look within his own ruling faculty, where true justice resides [274], but sought it in the corrupt offices of the attic courts [74]. He ran after advocates and low-level clerks, trying to bribe a system that had no power over him except what his own imagination surrendered to it [332]. In the end, he was executed like a dog because he lived like a puppet, pulled by the strings of social expectation [74, 278]."

On The Metamorphosis
For Gregor Samsa, the Emperor’s judgment would be even harsher, yet strangely fitting. He would recognize the opening of Book V of his Meditations as the literal, terrifying blueprint of Gregor’s regression. Gregor’s first thought upon waking as a giant insect was not of his soul, but of the train schedule, his sample cases, and his fear of his boss's anger [35, 87]. He preferred to lie under the warm covers, dreading the cold morning, whispering to himself that he must rise to a job he hated [1, 246].

The Emperor would say: "Gregor Samsa chose to become an animal long before his body transformed [31]. He was already a slave to his family’s debt, a wage slave who had no time to think, who defined his worth entirely by his ability to earn crowns for his parents [30, 35]. He atrophied his rational mind for the sake of his career [35, 246]. When his soul could no longer bear the tension, his body simply caught up with his spiritual state [43, 49]. His transformation into a vermin was the natural, physical consequence of his complete surrender to the flesh [49]. He spent his insect life hiding under a couch, weeping at his sister's violin music, and clinging to a picture of a lady in furs [43, 125, 133, 153]. He died because he could not master his own mind, and his family rejoiced at his death because they, too, are leaves blown down by the wind, ignorant of the Universal Reason [70, 266]."

V. The Codex of the Emperor: 20 Quotes Kafka Wishes He Had Written

Here, I record twenty passages from the Emperor’s hand that cut into my heart like glass. I have organized them by the themes that haunt my own sleepless nights, followed by my brief, whispered responses.

Theme: Suffering
1. "Such as bathing appears to thee,—oil, sweat, dirt, filthy water, all things disgusting,—so is every part of life and everything." (Book VIII, 24) [315, 391]
    Kafka's Diary:* Yes! This is the absolute reality of existence. We dress ourselves in fine wool, we carry walking sticks, we dandy ourselves in the cafes of Prague, but beneath it all is only "oil, sweat, filth." [82, 315] Life is a nauseous, heavy bath from which we cannot emerge clean.
2. "Turn it [the body] inside out, and see what kind of thing it is; and when it has grown old, what kind of thing it becomes, and when it is diseased." (Book VIII, 21) [314, 390]
    Kafka's Diary:* This is the anatomy of my own literature [85]. To write is to turn the human body inside out, to expose the raw nerves, the rottenness, the coughing of blood [108]. The dandy Kafka and the sick patient Kafka are the same creature [60, 108]. We are only bone and network [278].

Theme: Freedom
3. "Remember that to change thy opinion and to follow him who corrects thy error is as consistent with freedom as it is to persist in thy error." (Book VIII, 16) [389]
    Kafka's Diary:* How beautiful this sounds, yet how impossible. To submit to another is to admit defeat, yet in my fictions, absolute submission is the only way my characters can find a temporary, agonizing peace [20]. Karl Rossman, Gregor, Josef K.—their only freedom is the freedom of the condemned [26, 74].
4. "It is in our power to have no opinion about a thing, and not to be disturbed in our soul; for things themselves have no natural power to form our judgments." (Book VI, 52) [373]
    Kafka's Diary:* The Emperor has the luxury of choosing his opinions. But for me, the world does not wait for my judgment. The world is a circular saw that cuts through my bone; my opinion of the saw does not stop the blade. Yet, I wish I could believe this—even for three hours [299].

Theme: Isolation
5. "In a little space you will be only ashes and dry bones and a name, perhaps not even that... We are as puppies that snap at one another, as children that quarrel, laugh, and presently weep again." (Book V, 33) [248, 364]
    Kafka's Diary:* We are locked in our separate rooms, scratching at the doors [41, 94]. My family sits at the dinner table, and their voices reach me as an incomprehensible squeaking [49]. We snap at one another like little dogs, but our teeth never find the meat of the other's soul [96, 248].
6. "Accustom thyself to attend carefully to what is said by another, and as much as it is possible, be in the speaker's mind." (Book VI, 53) [250, 373]
    Kafka's Diary:* I have tried this my whole life, and it has only increased my estrangement [28]. To be in another’s mind is to realize how utterly empty their love for you is, how they look at you and see only a sick, useless cousin, a bachelor who will leave no heirs [85, 94].

Theme: Guilt
7. "Do wrong to thyself, do wrong to thyself, my soul; but thou wilt no longer have the opportunity of honoring thyself. Every man’s life is sufficient." (Book II, 6) [340]
    Kafka's Diary:* This is the signature of my existence. I am my own prosecutor, my own judge, and my own executioner [12, 98]. My soul has done nothing but "wrong to itself," and now the time is spent. The court is waiting [66, 74].
8. "I have often wondered how it is that every man loves himself more than all the rest of men, but yet sets less value on his own opinion of himself than on the opinion of others." (Book XII, 4) [327, 415]
    Kafka's Diary:* A devastating observation. Why do I care that Frau Grubach thinks my arrest is "learned"? [123] Why did Gregor Samsa worry about the chief clerk's opinion while his own voice was turning into an animal's whistle? [35, 49] We are parasites who feed on the thoughts of our victimizers [31].

Theme: Time
9. "Of human life the time is a point, and the substance is in a flux, and the perception dull... everything which belongs to the body is a stream, and what belongs to the soul is a dream and vapor." (Book II, 17) [282, 344]
    Kafka's Diary:* Our life is a brief, smoky point between two boundless eternities of darkness [295, 344]. We try to build castles, to write novels, to sign insurance policies, but the paper dissolves in our wet hands [68, 100, 106]. We are made of vapor, and we write in water [282].
10. "How quickly all things disappear,—in the universe the bodies themselves, but in time the remembrance of them." (Book II, 12) [281, 342]
     Kafka's Diary:* Max promises to publish my books after I die [82]. What an empty, ridiculous hope [82, 291]. Within a few generations, my name will sound as strange and antiquated as "Volesus" or "Leonnatus." [291] The earth will swallow my manuscripts, and the mold will eat my words [69, 291].

Theme: Bureaucracy
11. "Most of what we say and do is not essential. If you can eliminate it, you'll have more time, and more tranquility. Ask yourself at every moment, 'Is this necessary?'" (Book IV, 24) [2]
     Kafka's Diary:* If I could ask this of my office! The endless columns of risk percentages, the files, the letters of appeals [103]. My life has been plundered by the unessential [100, 109]. I have spent my best hours writing memos on wood-planing safety, while my true life—my writing—sat in the corner, starved of air [103, 109].
12. "Let no man any longer hear thee finding fault with the court life or with thy own." (Book VIII, 9) [313, 388]
     Kafka's Diary:* The "court life" of the Emperor and the "office life" of the clerk are the same [106]. I must stop complaining about the Workers' Accident Insurance Institute [100]. The office is my father; the office is the law; the office is the cage in which I must live until I die [106].

Theme: Death
13. "Since it is possible that thou mayest depart from life this very moment, regulate every act and thought accordingly." (Book II, 11) [280, 341]
     Kafka's Diary:* I carry this thought with me every time I cough [67]. Death is not a future event; it is the second hand on my watch [3]. It is the blood on my handkerchief [67]. If I am to write, I must write as if my hand is already being guided by the skeleton [109].
14. "Do not despise death, but be well content with it, since this too is one of those things which nature wills... like the child which leaves the womb." (Book IX, 3) [319, 398]
     Kafka's Diary:* To fall out of this envelope—what a deliverance! [319] My body is a sick, narrow cage [109]. If death is merely the soul falling out of its skin, then I shall welcome it as Gregor welcomed his final, quiet night when his head sank down and his breath escaped [70, 319].
15. "Constantly consider how many physicians are dead after often contracting their eyebrows over the sick; and how many astrologers... and how many philosophers..." (Book IV, 48) [294, 357]
     Kafka's Diary:* My doctors examine my throat, they inject alcohol into my larynx, they recommend sanatoria in Davos or Meran [111, 113]. They knit their brows and look solemn [294]. But they will all follow me into the same dirt [294, 357]. The clinic and the graveyard are only two rooms in the same building [111, 113].

Theme: Meaning
16. "He who has a vehement desire for posthumous fame does not consider that every one of those who remember him will himself also die very soon... complete oblivion soon buries them." (Book IV, 19) [353]
     Kafka's Diary:* I have instructed Max to burn everything [82]. Every scrap of paper, every letter, every diary page [82]. If my writing has any value, it is only as a private prayer, a desperate attempt to find a way out [109]. To seek "fame" is to ask for the applause of a crowd of ghosts [353].
17. "He who loves fame considers another man’s activity to be his own good... but he who has understanding considers his own acts to be his own good." (Book VI, 51) [373]
     Kafka's Diary:* My father's business, his robust health, his pride—these were his "good." [28, 95] He could not understand why I sat in my room, fasting and writing, refusing to become a partner in his asbestos factory [95, 109]. I must learn to find my good in my own ruin, which is my only true creation [109].

Theme: Resistance
18. "It is a shame for the soul to be first to give way in this life, when thy body does not give way." (Book VI, 29) [301, 370]
     Kafka's Diary:* In my case, it is the reverse. My soul is still screaming, still writing, still reaching for the stars, while my body—this miserable, tubercular shell—is collapsing beneath me [67, 108, 109]. My body is giving way first, and my soul must watch its own house burn down [108].
19. "Short is the little which remains to thee of life. Live as on a mountain... If they cannot endure him, let them kill him. For that is better than to live thus." (Book X, 15) [321, 406]
     Kafka's Diary:* Yes! It is better to die upright, or to be crushed by the system, than to live "thus"—as a hypocrite, a clerk who hates his office, a son who cringes before his father [95, 100]. If Prague cannot endure the artist Kafka, let it kill him [74, 94].

Theme: Acceptance
20. "The gale that blows from God we must endure, Toiling, but not repining." (Book VII, 51) [253]
     Kafka's Diary:* The "gale that blows from God" is the cold air of the void [28, 53]. We must toil in our burrow, we must dig our tunnels, we must write our fragments, even as the wind howls outside and the earth collapses around us [97]. We must endure, but we cannot help but weep [97].

*

VI. Epilogue: The Falling of the Olive

The candle is guttering. The ink in my well is turning cold. The Emperor closes his book with a prayer of thanksgiving to Universal Nature: "Depart contentedly. So the olive falls when it is grown ripe, blessing the ground from whence it sprung..." [294, 357]

How I envy his ripe olive [294]. When I fall, I shall not fall like a ripe fruit, blessing the earth [294]. I shall fall like a beetle that has been struck by an apple, or like a man who has been pushed into the river by his father’s voice [70, 95, 96]. My death will not be an operation of "Universal Reason"; it will be an unfinished sentence, a broken pen, a final, raspy gasp in a sanatorium room where there is no one but a maid who wants to clean the bedclothes [69, 113, 114].

Yet, I close this book with a strange gratitude. Marcus Aurelius did not solve the riddle of the world; he merely showed me that the cage is ancient [335, 421]. The Emperor was as much of a prisoner in his palace as I am in my office [1, 106]. He, too, sat in his tent, looking at his small, trembling hands, and wondered if he was only a dream from which he had not yet awaked [2, 226]. 

And so, we must keep writing, even if our books are only letters to ourselves that will be burned by those we love [82]. We must keep our "ruling part" awake, if only to observe the beauty of our own destruction [278, 422].

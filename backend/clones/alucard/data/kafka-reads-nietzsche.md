# Kafka Reads Nietzsche: A Diary of Vertigo and Venom

*Prague, October 18, 1922*

### I. The Hammer and the Pen: An Evening of Terror

Yesterday evening, Max lent me several heavy volumes of Friedrich Nietzsche [85, 86]. I sat with them until the lamp sputtered and died, leaving me in the grey, icy chill of dawn that always smells of coal-dust and my own failing lungs. My chest felt hollow, like an empty warehouse where some faceless clerk is forever dragging iron crates across the floor.

How does one read a man who writes with a hammer? One does not read him; one is struck by him. Each sentence is an execution, a beautiful, polished blade that falls upon the neck of our small, miserable comforts. I felt a terrifying vertigo as I turned the pages of *Thus Spake Zarathustra* and *Beyond Good and Evil* [87, 88]. Here is a man who stands upon the highest peaks, shouting into the wind, demanding that man surpass himself, that man become a lightning-bolt [188, 191]. And here am I, Franz Kafka, a minor bureaucrat at the Workers’ Accident Insurance Institute, a creature of the dark valley, who spends his days measuring the exact monetary value of a worker's severed finger and his nights shivering under three woollen blankets, trying to write a single sentence that is not a confession of guilt.

And yet, despite the chasm that separates this thundering German prophet from this sickly Jewish clerk, I felt a strange, poisonous communion with him. It is the communion of two recluses who have gone too deep into their own labyrinths and can no longer find their way back to the light of common men [15]. Nietzsche writes of the "noble solitude" [14], but I know only the solitude of the cellar, the silence of the insect that has crept behind the wainscoting to escape the heavy, boots of its father [40, 46]. He is the philosopher of the heights; I am the writer of the crawlspaces. But we both know the absolute, freezing temperature of the abyss [191, 284].

### II. The Resonances: The Architecture of Our Shared Prison

Several of Nietzsche's most terrifying diagnoses strike me not as philosophical theories, but as the literal blueprints of my own life.

#### 1. The Inward Turn of Guilt
In *The Genealogy of Morals*, Nietzsche describes how the animal instincts of man, when blocked from outward discharge by the walls of society, turn inward, creating the "bad conscience" [88, 145]. This is the most profound thing I have ever read. He calls it the "self-crucifixion" of two thousand years, the "vivisection of conscience" [165]. For me, this is not a historical thesis; it is my daily bread. My guilt is not a reaction to any specific deed; it is my very substance. I am always in the wrong [8]. 

My father, Hermann Kafka, was the robust, healthy animal who discharged his strength outwardly—shouting, slamming doors, commanding the household with a terrifying, unthinking confidence [40]. And I, the sickly, delicate child, had to swallow my responses, to store them in my blood where they fermented into a permanent self-prosecution. My writing is nothing but this inward-turned hostility, a legal trial that has been running in my head since childhood, where I am simultaneously the accused, the courtroom audience, and the executioner.

#### 2. The Cold Monster of the Machine
Zarathustra's cry against the State—"the coldest of all cold monsters" [8, 223]—resonated through my very bones. In my office at the Insurance Institute, I see this cold monster every day. It does not hate the workers; it does not love them. It simply translates their living, bleeding flesh into neat, bloodless files, columns of figures, and appeals. It is a machine that runs on its own logic, a law that is written on the body of the condemned without them ever being permitted to know what the law is. 

When Nietzsche writes that "the herd-instinct of obedience is transmitted best, and at the cost of the art of command" [23], I see the faces of my fellow clerks, and I see my own pale, obedient face in the mirror. We are the "Last Men" who "have discovered happiness" and blink [193, 195]. We have our little pleasures for the day, and our little pleasures for the night, but we have a regard for health [195]. We are pieces of a machine that has forgotten who built it, running in a void.

#### 3. Profound Suffering and the Mask
Nietzsche writes that "profound suffering makes noble: it separates" [27]. He says that "every profound spirit needs a mask" to protect itself from the vulgar, prying hands of the sympathetic [17, 27]. How deeply I know this need for concealment! My entire life in Prague is a system of masks. I play the polite, cheerful clerk; I write long, agonizingly detailed letters to Felice, which are themselves masks designed to keep her at a distance, to prevent her from ever seeing the cold, dry ruin of my inner being. 

My laughter—which Max says is delightful—is often nothing but the desperate rattling of a wooden partition that keeps my terror from spilling over. We are "gay men who make use of gaiety, because we are misunderstood on account of it" [27]. We wish to be misunderstood, because to be understood would be to be touched, and to be touched is to be destroyed.

### III. The Rejections: The Cruelty of the Hammer

And yet, there are moments when I must lay Nietzsche's books down with a feeling of profound disgust and rejection. His philosophy is a magnificent, gold-plated carriage that is built only for those who are already strong enough to walk on their own. For the ruined, the sickly, the deformed—for the Gregor Samsas of this world—it is nothing but a wheels-of-iron machine that crushes us into the dirt.

#### 1. The Delusion of the Will to Power
Nietzsche asserts that "Life is Will to Power" [12, 130, 157]. He claims that even in the servant, there is the will to be master [130, 258], and that "all active force" is defined by this single, dominating drive [16, 176]. But I know a reality that is far more terrible: the Will to Submit, the Will to Disappear, the Will to become an insect so that one may finally be exempt from the struggle of existence [42]. 

My father did not represent a "noble" Will to Power; he represented a brutal, blind, physiological weight that crushed everything around him [40]. If all life is nothing but the "appropriation, injury, overpowering of what is alien and weaker" [15, 31], then life is a nightmare from which there is no awakening. Nietzsche mocks "the fatalism of the weak-willed" as a form of bad taste [13]. But for those of us whose lungs are rotting, whose wills are paralyzed by a thousand doubts, this is not "bad taste"—it is our physical reality. We do not have "strong wills" or "weak wills" [13]; we have bodies that are being systematically dismantled by a disease that does not care about our "Will to Power" [124].

#### 2. The Übermensch as a Cruel Chimera
The Übermensch is Zarathustra's "lightning out of the dark cloud" [191, 199]. He demands that we leap over man, that we treat man as "something that is to be surpassed" [188, 210, 308]. But why must we leap? We have not even succeeded in *being* human yet. We are like children who try to run before they can crawl, who fall and break their knees upon the stones. 

For me, the distance between man and the simplest, most basic human tasks—marriage, fatherhood, simple communion with another human being [40]—is already infinite. To hold up the Übermensch as an ideal to a man who cannot even sleep at night without being choked by his own thoughts is a form of spiritual cruelty. It is "weariness, which seeketh to get to the ultimate with one leap, with a death-leap" [206]. It is a magnificent fantasy built by a man who was himself collapsing into madness, a mask of health worn by a dying soul [186, 338].

#### 3. The Lie of Amor Fati
Nietzsche demands that we love our fate, that we say "Yea" to everything, that we desire the eternal recurrence of our exact, agonizing lives [139, 314]. "Was that life? Well! Once more!" [288, 373]. I shudder at the very thought. To love one's tuberculosis? To love the fact that I spent my life in a cubicle, writing appeals for a state that is a cold monster? To love the fact that I could never marry the woman I loved because my conscience was a minotaur that tore me piecemeal? [15] 

No, this is not "redemption" [385]; it is a terrible form of self-hypnosis [148, 153]. One cannot love fate; one can only endure it, like a beast that has been beaten so often that it no longer even raises its head when the whip cracks [121]. Nietzsche's "Amor Fati" is the ultimate triumph of the executioner: he has managed to convince the condemned man to kiss the blade of the axe [165].

### IV. The Dialogue of the Judges: Nietzsche Reads Kafka

How would Friedrich Nietzsche, this proud "immoralist" [24, 155], read my own poor, tortured stories? If he were to sit in judgment over *The Trial* and *The Metamorphosis*, his verdict would be swift, cold, and utterly devoid of pity.

#### 1. On *The Trial*
Nietzsche would see Josef K. not as a tragic victim, but as a classic specimen of "slave-morality" and physiological decadence [160, 378]. He would say:

> "Look at this Josef K.! He is arrested on his thirtieth birthday, and what does he do? He does not fight; he does not draw a sword; he does not even ask what the law is. Instead, he wanders through the filthy, dark attics of the court, seeking judges, seeking advocates, seeking some higher authority to tell him whether he is guilty or innocent [142]. He is a man who cannot command himself, and therefore he must obey [257]. 
>
> His guilt is not an objective fact; it is a symptom of his weakened instincts, his inability to be the author of his own values [141, 161]. He goes to his execution like a sheep to the slaughter, voluntarily offering his neck to the knife [210]. He dies 'like a dog' because he lived like a dog—a member of the herd who lost his 'pathos of distance' and allowed himself to be hypnotised by the shadow of an imaginary court [153, 389]. K.'s execution is not an injustice; it is the natural, healthy function of life purging itself of a degenerate member who was too weak to live [140]."

#### 2. On *The Metamorphosis*
Nietzsche would read Gregor Samsa’s transformation into a giant insect with a mixture of scientific curiosity and deep contempt [144, 243]. He would diagnose it as the physical crystallization of Gregor's spiritual surrender:

> "Gregor Samsa is the ultimate 'Last Man' [193]. He spends his life running after trains, slave to a petty firm, carrying his family's debts, thinking only of his security and his health [42, 195]. He has no 'Will to Power' [142]; he has only the will to conform. He has turned himself into an instrument of the herd [23]. His physical transformation into a beetle is not a curse from without; it is his own body's honest, biological reaction to his spiritual degradation [152, 209]. His body says: 'You have lived like an insect, now look like one!' 
>
> But even as an insect, Gregor cannot rid himself of his slave-conscience [145]. He is ashamed of his form; he hides under the sofa; he worries about his family's finances [42, 46]. He has 'the sickness unto death'—the despair of weakness [184, 185]. And when his father throws the apple at him—that robust, healthy animal of a father who asserts his dominance—Gregor does not resist. He allows the apple to rot in his back, accepting his own destruction as his duty to the herd [46]. Gregor's death is the only clean thing in the book; it is the 'fatality' of the weak-willed executing itself [140]."

### V. The Codex of the Sovereign: Twenty Quotes from Nietzsche

Here are twenty sentences from the actual texts of the Emperor of Sils-Maria [32], which I have copied down into my diary. They are like twenty iron nails driven into my coffin. I have tagged each with its true, bleeding theme, and whispered my response to each from the dark of my room.

#### 1. Guilt
> "I did that, says my memory. 'I could not have done that,' says my pride, and remains inexorable. Eventually—the memory yields."  
> — *Beyond Good and Evil*, IV. 68 [19]  

*Franz's Response*: My memory has yielded entirely to my father's pride. When I remember my childhood, I do not see what actually happened; I see only his towering, robust figure, and my own small, guilty shadow, already pre-convicted in his eyes [40].

#### 2. Bureaucracy
> "A state, is called the coldest of all cold monsters. Coldly lieth it also; and this lie creeps from its mouth: 'I, the state, am the people.'"  
> — *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, I. XI [223]  

*Franz's Response*: Yes, the state is a cold monster, but its clerks are colder still. We sit in our heated offices, writing the files that decide the fate of men we will never see, pretending that our bloodless codes are the voice of justice [8].

#### 3. Isolation
> "Every profound spirit needs a mask; nay, more, around every profound spirit there continually grows a mask, owing to the constantly false, that is to say, superficial interpretation of every word he utters..."  
> — *Beyond Good and Evil*, II. 40 [17]  

*Franz's Response*: My mask is so thick that I can no longer feel my own face beneath it. When I speak to my mother, when I write to Felice, I hear a voice that is not mine—a polite, reasonable voice that has been constructed to hide the beetle that is shivering inside [42, 46].

#### 4. Suffering
> "The discipline of suffering, of great suffering—know ye not that it is only this discipline that has produced all the elevations of humanity hitherto?"  
> — *Beyond Good and Evil*, VII. 225 [24]  

*Franz's Response*: My tuberculosis is my discipline. My cough is the sound of the hammer striking the stone [176, 252]. Without this physical ruin, this constant sense of suffocation, I would have nothing to write. My literature is not a gift; it is a symptom of my disease [152].

#### 5. Freedom
> "It is the business of the very few to be independent; it is a privilege of the strong."  
> — *Beyond Good and Evil*, II. 29 [15]  

*Franz's Response*: I tried to be independent. I broke my engagements; I lived alone in a cold room in Prague. But my freedom was a desert [229]. I was not strong enough for it; I was torn piecemeal by the minotaur of my own conscience [15].

#### 6. Guilt
> "Terrible is aloneness with the judge and avenger of one's own law. Thus is a star projected into desert space, and into the icy breath of aloneness."  
> — *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, I. XVII [229]  

*Franz's Response*: This is my night-watches. I sit alone at my desk, and the room becomes a windowless court. There is no judge from without; I am my own accuser, my own advocate, and my own executioner [142, 229].

#### 7. Time
> "'It was': thus is the Will's teeth-gnashing and lonesomest tribulation called. Impotent towards what hath been done—it is a malicious spectator of all that is past."  
> — *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, II. XLII [271]  

*Franz's Response*: My life was decided before I was born [81, 117]. The "It was" is a stone that I can never roll away [272]. I spent my childhood trying to earn my father's love; that past is an iron gate that has been locked forever, and I am left gnashing my teeth outside [40].

#### 8. Bureaucracy
> "Whatever cannot obey itself, is commanded. Such is the nature of living things."  
> — *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, II. XXXIV [257]  

*Franz's Response*: Because I could never command my own body, because I could never make a simple decision without trembling, I had to enter the Insurance Institute [42]. I had to be commanded by a clock, by a manager, by a file [103]. I am a natural servant who has been given a clerk's pen [130, 258].

#### 9. Isolation
> "Profound suffering makes noble: it separates."  
> — *Beyond Good and Evil*, IX. 270 [27]  

*Franz's Response*: It separates, yes, but not like a king on his throne [306]. It separates like a patient in a sanatorium, behind a glass screen, watching the healthy people walk in the garden outside. It is a nobility of the dying [140, 297].

#### 10. Resistance
> "Not by wrath, but by laughter, do we slay. Come, let us slay the spirit of gravity!"  
> — *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, I. VII [214]  

*Franz's Response*: I tried to laugh. When I read the first chapter of *The Trial* to Max, we laughed until the tears ran down our cheeks. But our laughter was a thin, fragile membrane stretched over a bottomless, seething pit of despair [56, 74].

#### 11. Death
> "One should do honour to the fatality which says to the feeble: 'perish!'"  
> — *The Will to Power*, I. 140 [140]  

*Franz's Response*: My lungs are performing this duty for me. They say 'perish' every time I cough. I do not struggle against this fatality; I wait for it like a child waiting for the end of a long, exhausting school-day [80, 115].

#### 12. Isolation
> "It is difficult to live among men because silence is so difficult."  
> — *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, II. XXV [245]  

*Franz's Response*: Yes, the noise of their voices, their questions, their expectations [121]. My letters are nothing but an attempt to maintain a silence while pretending to speak, to build a wall of words between myself and those who love me [17].

#### 13. Meaning
> "In man creature and creator are united: in man there is not only matter, shred, excess, clay, mire, folly, chaos; but there is also the creator..."  
> — *Beyond Good and Evil*, VII. 225 [24]  

*Franz's Response*: I am the raw, bleeding clay, the mire and the chaos [24]. But when I write, I must also be the hard sculptor who strikes his own flesh with the hammer. To write is to be both the victim on the altar and the priest with the knife [252, 257].

#### 14. Guilt
> "The voice of the herd will still echo in thee. And when thou sayest, 'I have no longer a conscience in common with you,' then will it be a plaint and a pain."  
> — *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, I. XVII [228]  

*Franz's Response*: I tried to leave the synagogue, to leave my family's middle-class German-Jewish herd [40]. But their conscience still echoes in my bones [228]. Every time I write a line, I hear their voices asking: 'Why are you not married? Why do you not have children? Why do you not work like a man?' [220]

#### 15. Time
> "Everything goeth, everything returneth; eternally rolleth the wheel of existence... eternally true to itself remaineth the ring of existence."  
> — *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, III. LVII [314]  

*Franz's Response*: The most terrifying thought in the universe [318]. To think that this exact room, this sputtering lamp, this cold coffee, and this rotting lung will return again and again throughout eternity, and I will always sit here, writing this same diary entry, with no hope of ever escaping into nothingness [122, 321].

#### 16. Resistance
> "Spirit is life which itself cutteth into life: by its own torture doth it increase its own knowledge,—did ye know that before?"  
> — *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, II. XXX [252]  

*Franz's Response*: Writing is this cutting. I must use my own living spirit as a knife to dissect my own life [252]. Every story I write is a piece of my own flesh that I have laid upon the table for the readers to devour [25, 26].

#### 17. Meaning
> "The noble soul has reverence for itself."  
> — *Beyond Good and Evil*, IX. 287 [29]  

*Franz's Response*: I have no reverence for myself. I have only disgust [210]. I see myself as a parasite, a beetle, a piece of wood that has been left to rot in the mud [42, 46]. If nobility requires self-reverence, then I am the lowest of the low [210, 212].

#### 18. Death
> "The God who beheld everything, and also man: that God had to die! Man cannot endure it that such a witness should live."  
> — *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, IV. LXVII [351]  

*Franz's Response*: My father was this God [40]. He saw every secret weakness, every petty lie, every shiver of my body. To live under his gaze was to be suffocated [351]. He had to die, yet his death has left me with a guilt that is heavier than his life [158, 212].

#### 19. Acceptance
> "Woe saith: 'Hence! Go! Away, thou woe!' But everything that suffereth wanteth to live, that it may become mature and lively and longing..."  
> — *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, IV. LXXIX [374]  

*Franz's Response*: At night, when the pain in my chest is like a hot iron, my soul cries out for death [98, 113]. Yet, when the morning comes and a single ray of sun falls upon my desk, I clutch my pen and want to live, if only to write one more true sentence [373].

#### 20. Acceptance
> "Joy, however, doth not want heirs, it doth not want children,—joy wanteth itself, it wanteth eternity, it wanteth recurrence, it wanteth everything eternally-like-itself."  
> — *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, IV. LXXIX [374]  

*Franz's Response*: I know no such joy. My only joy is the cold, pale moment of relief when a sentence is completed, when the self-prosecution is temporarily suspended, and the courtroom goes quiet for a single minute [152, 185].

### VI. Epilogue: The Silence of the Valley

Nietzsche ended his Zarathustra with the great noontide, with the lion and the children, with a morning sun coming out of gloomy mountains [376]. 

But I am writing this in the dark of Prague, and my day is ending before it has even begun [265]. I do not have a lion's voice [281]. I have only this scratching pen. 

When I die, my body will not be scattered to the winds of the Engadine [32, 104]. I will be buried in the Jewish cemetery at Olšany, under a heavy, grey stone, next to the father whose gaze I could never escape [40]. 

Nietzsche's hammer has broken the old tables, but it has left us with nothing but a heap of beautiful, sharp gold-shards that cut our feet when we try to walk [17, 308]. We are left in the valley, sherd-creatures, crawling through the ruins, waiting for the cold wind of the night to blow us away [191, 198].

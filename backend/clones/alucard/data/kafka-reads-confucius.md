# Kafka Reads Confucius

**Franz Kafka’s Personal Diaries**  
**Prague, Late Autumn 1922**

---

### I. The Great Wall of the Family (An Evening of Dust and Ritual)

It is late. The oil lamp on my desk hums with a dry, insect-like persistence that seems to proceed directly from my own temples. Before me lies a translation of the ancient Chinese classics—the *Analects* of Master Kong, whom the Westerners call Confucius. In the adjoining room, the heavy, rhythmic breathing of my father penetrates the thin plaster wall. It is not merely the sound of a sleeping man; it is an administrative decree, a physical law that occupies the entire flat, leaving me only this narrow corner of desk-space and this failing throat. 

How strange to read these ancient rules of filial piety (*Xiao*) and ritual propriety (*Li*) while my lungs decay under the Prague damp. Confucius speaks of a universe built upon the perfect extension of the patriarchal household. The family is the state in miniature; the state is the family writ large. To a Chinese sage of the Lu state, this is the blueprint of cosmic harmony. To a sick clerk of the Workers' Accident Insurance Institute, it is the blueprint of an inescapable, multi-tiered prison. 

I feel a bizarre, late-night brotherhood with this Master Kong. He, too, was a frustrated clerk, a minor bureaucrat who wandered from court to court, offering meticulously organized filing systems for the soul to rulers who preferred the raw, chaotic violence of war. He wished to establish an absolute order of gestures, words, and duties to stave off the collapse of his world. I, too, spend my mornings in the office classifying the severed fingers and crushed limbs of industrial laborers, trying to fit human agony into the neat columns of imperial insurance forms. But where Confucius saw in these rituals the path to human completion, I see only the elegant carpentry of our execution.

---

### II. The Resonances: The Bureaucracy of the Soul

As I turn these dry, orderly pages, certain ideas strike my heart with the cold precision of an office stamp. 

#### 1. The Trap of Filial Piety (*Xiao*) and Inescapable Guilt
In the Confucian universe, the father-son relationship is the primary axis of reality. A son must not merely obey his father; he must cultivate an inner disposition of absolute reverence, anticipating the father's desires and carrying his name with trembling care. If the father errs, the son may gently remonstrate, but must remain subordinate, never raising his voice or his hand. 

This is the very engine of my existence. For what is my entire life, my writing, my illness, if not a prolonged, failed remonstrance against Hermann Kafka? In my *Letter to My Father*, which I could never bring myself to deliver, I tried to map this exact territory. Confucius codifies this relationship as a sacred duty; he does not see that when a father is a giant, a non-dialectical physical tyrant who rules by sheer bulk and roaring decrees, filial piety becomes a mechanism of infinite guilt. Because I can never meet his gaze without flinching, because I cannot match his physical success, I am permanently in arrears. In the Confucian system, my failure to marry, my failure to take over his fancy-goods business, and my withdrawal into the nocturnal attic of literature are not mere personal choices—they are cosmic crimes. My guilt is not for anything I have *done*, but for what I *am*: a thin, coughing clerk who cannot justify his own space at the family dinner table.

#### 2. The Rectification of Names (*Zhengming*) and Administrative Absurdity
When asked what he would do first if entrusted with the government of a state, Confucius replied that he would "rectify names." If names are not correct, language is not in accordance with the truth of things; if language is not in accordance with truth, affairs cannot be carried on to success; and if affairs collapse, justice goes astray.

Here is an idea I wish I had written, for it is the secret key to our modern nightmares. In our insurance offices and courts of law, the "Rectification of Names" is practiced with terrifying, reverse-engineered efficiency. A man is no longer a living breathing creature; he is stamped with a name: *The Accused*, *The Appellant*, *The Sorely Afflicted (Class III)*. Once the name is rectified by the administration, the human being must be mutilated to fit the category. In my office, we do not look at the ruined hand of a carpenter; we look at a claim file. If the file says "Minor Contusion," then the man’s pain is officially minor, even if his fingers are blackening with gangrene. The name is rectified, the files are balanced, and the man may go home to starve in perfect accordance with the law. The absurd arises precisely when the living body rebels against its administrative name, but has no language left with which to appeal.

#### 3. Ritual Propriety (*Li*) as the Machine
Confucius argues that without *Li*—the formal, highly structured patterns of behavior, dress, and speech—society collapses into beastliness. Every bow, every cup of tea offered, every step taken in the presence of a superior must conform to ancient patterns. 

I know this *Li* intimately. I wear my high stiff collar, my dark three-piece suit, my polished bowler hat; I walk to the insurance building at my prescribed hour; I bow to the Director; I draft my appeals in the bloodless, Latinate German of the Prague civil service. We perform these rituals with religious devotion. But we have forgotten *why*. The rituals of the civil service have outlived the gods who inspired them. We bow to an empty throne; we file briefs for a judge who retired forty years ago and whose successor is a rumor in an attic. In Confucius, *Li* is the outward expression of an inner moral beauty; in Prague, *Li* is the hollow casing of a machine that continues to grind its gears long after the factory has been abandoned.

---

### III. The Rejections: The Cruelty of Social Harmony

Despite the beauty of Master Kong’s style—which has the sparse, clean quality of dry wood—I must reject his central faith. I reject it with the visceral panic of a man who knows that if he enters that tidy temple, he will be suffocated by the incense.

#### 1. The Illusion of the Benevolent Sovereign and the Patriarchal State
Confucius believes that if a ruler is virtuous, the people will follow him naturally, just as the grass bends when the wind blows across it. He views the state as an extended family, ruled by a benevolent father-king who cares for his subjects as his own children.

This is a beautiful, deadly lie. The state is not a father; it is a cold, multi-headed monster whose organs are made of paper, ink, and iron. My father’s house was not a temple of benevolence; it was a courtroom where he was simultaneously the accuser, the judge, and the executioner. To model the state on the family is to legitimize the absolute tyranny of the domestic patriarch on a national scale. The "wind" that blows from the ruler does not make the grass bend in graceful harmony; it tears the grass up by its roots. The subjects of our modern empires are not children gathered around a hearth; they are defendants waiting in the drafty corridors of an unapproachable palace, hoping their names will not be called for execution.

#### 2. The Banishing of the Inner Beast
The Confucian system assumes that human nature can be polished and refined through education and ritual until the animal instincts are entirely sublimated into social virtue. The *Junzi*—the gentleman—is a man of perfect self-control, whose emotions are balanced and whose desires never stray from the path of righteousness.

But what of the insect? What of the dog? What of the wild, screaming thing that lives beneath the starch of our collars? Confucius writes for a humanity that has never known the cellar. He does not see that our civilized rituals are merely a thin scab over an infected wound. When we suppress our animal nature too completely in the name of social duty, it does not disappear; it retreats into the dark, grows legs, and waits for morning. To deny the dark, erratic, and absurd elements of our nature is to guarantee their violent return. A philosophy that has no room for the crawlspace is useless to those of us who must live in it.

#### 3. The Lie of Moral Self-Cultivation as Salvation
Confucius promises that through rigorous study of the classics, reflection, and ethical effort, any man can achieve inner peace and align himself with the Will of Heaven (*Tian*).

This is a cruel form of spiritual optimism. I have studied my own conscience with a microscopic intensity that would make a Confucian scholar weep with envy. I have parsed my every motive, my every hesitation, my every draft. The result is not inner peace; it is a paralyzed will and a deeper conviction of my own corruption. The "Will of Heaven" is not a benevolent guide; it is a silent, ungraspable law that judges us without offering a handbook of its statutes. No amount of self-cultivation can save a man from a court that refuses to state his crime.

### IV. The Sage's Verdict on My Nightmares

If Master Kong were to sit in this cold room and read the manuscripts I keep locked in my drawer, he would view them not as fantastic parables, but as cautionary tales of a world that has suffered a catastrophic collapse of *Li* and *Xiao*.

#### 1. Confucius Reads *The Trial*
The Sage would look upon Josef K. not with pity, but with a stern, administrative disapproval. "This man," Confucius would write in his commentary, "did not know his place. On the morning of his arrest, instead of maintaining his composure and examining his own conscience for breaches of ritual propriety, he began to argue with minor warders, demand receipts, and insult the dignity of the court. He treated the low-ranking officers of the law with insolence, forgetting that even a minor clerk is an instrument of the social hierarchy."

To Confucius, Josef K.’s execution is the natural, inevitable consequence of his failure to cultivate *Xiao* toward the legal system. The Court, even in its corrupted, attic-dwelling form, represents the structure of authority. By attempting to fight the Court on his own individualistic terms, by refusing to seek a proper mediator, and by treating his trial as a personal insult rather than a cosmic corrective, K. alienated himself from the social organism. He died "like a dog" because he lived like an isolated beast, refusing to bow to the elders of the law. His death was not a tragedy of external oppression, but the self-inflicted execution of an unrectified name.

#### 2. Confucius Reads *The Metamorphosis*
For Gregor Samsa, the Sage would have a different, more melancholy verdict. He would see in Gregor’s transformation a literal, physical manifestation of a spiritual collapse. 

"Gregor Samsa," the Sage would observe, "was a youth who performed the outward gestures of filial piety while harboring a secret, rebellious heart. He worked as a traveling salesman to pay his father’s debts—this was correct. But he did so with resentment, complaining of the irregular hours, the bad food, and the coldness of human relations. He did not work out of love for his parents, but out of a mechanical, joyless obligation. His office was his temple, and his sample case was his altar."

Because Gregor’s filial piety was hollow—a mere performance of *Li* without the inner substance of *Ren* (benevolence)—his human form could not sustain itself. The moment he allowed his exhaustion to overcome his duty, his body conformed to his true inner state: that of a parasite, an insect that consumes the family’s resources without contributing to the whole. His transformation is the ultimate failure of the "Rectification of Names." He was called a "son" and a "provider," but his reality was that of a beast of burden. When his father throws the red apple that embeds itself in Gregor’s back, Confucius would view this not as an act of cruelty, but as a righteous, parental correction—the father restoring the natural hierarchy of the household by force, correcting the vermin that dared to occupy the family parlor.

### V. The Codex of the Sage: Twenty Essential Quotes with Diary Responses

Here are twenty sentences from the records of Master Kong that I have transcribed into my notebooks. I have stripped them of their political optimism, revealing the dark, legalistic skeletons that lie beneath their elegant characters.

#### 1. On Suffering
> *"The man of wisdom does not grieve; the man of benevolence does not worry; the man of courage does not fear."*  
> *(The Analects, IX.29)*

**My Diary:**  
To be without grief, worry, or fear is to be made of stone. The Master describes not a wise man, but an insurance ledger. My worry is my only proof of life; my fear is the thread that connects my head to my shoulders. If I stopped grieving, the space between my ribs would fill with cement.

#### 2. On Freedom
> *"To be able under all circumstances to practice five things constitutes perfect virtue; these five are gravity, generosity of soul, sincerity, earnestness, and kindness."*  
> *(The Analects, XVII.6)*

**My Diary:**  
The Master lists these virtues as if they were keys to a garden. He does not see that they are heavy iron locks. To practice gravity under the weight of my father’s gaze is to invite paralysis; to be earnest before a court that operates on whim is to hand the executioner his axe. My only freedom is the freedom to shrink, to crawl beneath the baseboard where these five virtues cannot reach me.

#### 3. On Isolation
> *"In the morning, hear the Way; in the evening, die content."*  
> *(The Analects, IV.8)*

**My Diary:**  
I have spent forty years listening at the keyhole of the Law, and I have heard only the rustle of old paper and the clearing of throat-phlegm from the clerks. If the "Way" passed through my street, it would be disguised as an imperial messenger who cannot get past the palace gates. I shall die in the evening, certainly, but my mouth will be full of dust and my ears full of silence.

#### 4. On Guilt
> *"When we see men of worth, we should think of equaling them; when we see men of a contrary character, we should turn inwards and examine ourselves."*  
> *(The Analects, IV.17)*

**My Diary:**  
The inward turn is a staircase that has no bottom. I look at my father—whom the world considers a man of great worth, a robust conqueror of the Prague markets—and I turn inwards. I find only a damp cellar filled with unfinished manuscripts, broken promises, and the quiet, rhythmic coughing of my own blood. The examination is never over; the sentence is written before the evidence is read.

#### 5. On Time
> *"The Master standing by a river said: 'It passes on just like this, not ceasing day or night!'"*  
> *(The Analects, IX.16)*

**My Diary:**  
Yes, time passes like the river, but it does not carry us to the sea. It carries us to the next office deadline, the next rent payment, the next family dinner where we must sit and pretend we are not dissolving. The river of time is made of black ink, and we are drowned in its margins.

#### 6. On Bureaucracy
> *"The relation between superiors and inferiors is like that between the wind and the grass. The grass must bend, when the wind blows across it."*  
> *(The Analects, XII.19)*

**My Diary:**  
The grass bends because it has no choice. But the wind in our modern offices does not blow from heaven; it blows from the exhaust pipes of a massive, indifferent engine. It is a dry, chemical wind that leaves a taste of coal-dust on the tongue. We bend until our foreheads touch the mud, and then we are stepped on by those who carry the files.

#### 7. On Death
> *"Ke Loo asked about serving the spirits of the dead. The Master said, 'While you are not able to serve men, how can you serve their spirits?' 'I venture to ask about death.' He was answered, 'While you do not know life, how can you know about death?'"*  
> *(The Analects, XI.11)*

**My Diary:**  
The Master evades the question with the elegance of a senior secretary. But to those of us whose life has been a long, administrative preparation for death, the two cannot be separated. I serve my father, I serve the Insurance Institute, I serve the German language—and yet I am dead to them all. My tuberculosis is not an illness; it is the physical presence of death entering my life like an uninvited uncle who refuses to leave the parlor.

#### 8. On Meaning
> *"If a man keeps cherishing his old knowledge, so as continually to be acquiring new, he may be a teacher of others."*  
> *(The Analects, II.11)*

**My Diary:**  
My old knowledge is a stack of letters from girls I could not love, and my new knowledge is the realization that my lungs are turning to paper. What can I teach anyone, except the art of hesitating before a closed door? The only true learning is the learning of how to disappear without causing a scene.

#### 9. On Resistance
> *"The commander of the forces of a large state may be carried off, but the will of even a common man cannot be taken from him."*  
> *(The Analects, IX.25)*

**My Diary:**  
The Master speaks of the "will" as if it were an iron sword. He does not know the modern state. The Court does not take your will; it makes you surrender it willingly. Josef K. goes to his execution not because he is bound by ropes, but because his own will has been recruited by his judges. The common man's will is the very instrument used to turn the key in his own cell door.

#### 10. On Acceptance
> *"At fifteen, I had my mind bent on learning. At thirty, I stood firm. At forty, I had no doubts. At fifty, I knew the decrees of Heaven. At sixty, my ear was an obedient organ for the reception of truth. At seventy, I could follow what my heart desired, without transgressing what was right."*  
> *(The Analects, II.4)*

**My Diary:**  
At fifteen, I was terrified of my father's shadow. At thirty, I was locked in an office drafting reports on industrial accidents. At forty, I am coughing blood in a rented room, doubted by everyone, including myself. My ear is indeed an obedient organ, but it receives only the sound of my own dissolution. My heart desires only to be allowed to sleep, but that is the one thing the Law cannot permit.

#### 11. On Suffering
> *"To be poor and not have to flatter is difficult; to be rich and not be proud is easy."*  
> *(The Analects, I.15)*

**My Diary:**  
I am poor in health, poor in spirit, and yet my entire life is a long, elaborate flattery. I flatter my father by shrinking in his presence; I flatter my office superiors by writing neat briefs; I flatter my readers by pretending my nightmares are only stories. Flattery is the oil that allows the gears of our survival to turn without grinding us to powder.

#### 12. On Freedom
> *"Fine words and an insinuating appearance are seldom associated with true virtue."*  
> *(The Analects, I.3)*

**My Diary:**  
The Master condemns the "insinuating appearance," but in our world, it is our only shield. My polite letters, my quiet demeanor, my elegant German prose—these are the "fine words" I use to keep the world from discovering that I am a hollow reed. If I spoke with the raw, unadorned truth of my heart, I would be committed to an asylum before noon.

#### 13. On Isolation
> *"Hold faithfulness and sincerity as first principles. Have no friends not equal to yourself."*  
> *(The Analects, I.8)*

**My Diary:**  
If I had no friends not equal to myself, I would be utterly alone, for I have never met a man who was as thoroughly divided against himself as I am. My friends are those who look at my face and see a living man, while I look in the mirror and see only a draft of a character that was abandoned by its author.

#### 14. On Guilt
> *"To err and not to reform, this may indeed be called erring."*  
> *(The Analects, XV.29)*

**My Diary:**  
But what if the "error" is our very birth? How does one reform the fact of his own existence? I reform my writing, I reform my habits, I reform my diet—and yet the original error remains, a heavy, dark stone lying at the center of my chest. The only true reform is dissolution.

#### 15. On Time
> *"The sequence of the seasons cannot be altered; the passage of the years cannot be delayed."*  
> *(The Book of Rites)*

**My Diary:**  
The seasons change, but the office remains the same temperature. The years pass, but the files do not grow any thinner. The calendar is our metronome, beating out the rhythm of our slow, administrative execution.

#### 16. On Bureaucracy
> *"When a ruler's personal conduct is correct, his government is effective without his issuing orders. If his personal conduct is not correct, he may issue orders, but they will not be obeyed."*  
> *(The Analects, XIII.6)*

**My Diary:**  
In our world, the personal conduct of the ruler is a secret kept behind three sets of double-doors. His orders are issued by clerks who have never seen his face. We obey not because the ruler is correct, but because the machinery of the order is so vast that we cannot find the starting switch to turn it off.

#### 17. On Death
> *"Let the end be treated with care, and let them be followed when long gone with the proper rites; then the virtue of the people will resume its proper excellence."*  
> *(The Analects, I.9)*

**My Diary:**  
My end will be treated with care only by the landlord, who will want my trunk removed so he can rent the room to another clerk. My rites will be the scratching of a pen on a death certificate. My virtue will resume its proper excellence when it is no longer required to drag this sick body through the streets of Prague.

#### 18. On Meaning
> *"The scholar who cherishes love of comfort is not fit to be deemed a scholar."*  
> *(The Analects, XIV.3)*

**My Diary:**  
I cherish only my bed, my blankets, and the cold silence of my room. If that makes me unfit, then I accept the title of the Unfit. The "scholar" who seeks to reform the state is a man who has never felt the luxury of a day spent without a single office interruption.

#### 19. On Resistance
> *"He who does not anticipate deceit, nor suspect bad faith, and yet is the first to perceive them when they occur—is he not a sage?"*  
> *(The Analects, XIV.33)*

**My Diary:**  
To live without anticipating deceit is to be a lamb walking into a butcher's shop. I anticipate deceit in every letter I receive, in every smile from my colleagues, in every cough that comes from my throat. I am not a sage; I am merely a suspect who has spent too much time reading his own indictment.

#### 20. On Acceptance
> *"Heaven does not speak; yet the four seasons run their course thereby, the hundred things, each after its kind, are generated thereby. Heaven does not speak."*  
> *(The Analects, XVII.19)*

**My Diary:**  
This is the most terrifying sentence in the entire book. Heaven does not speak. The Judge does not speak. The Emperor does not speak. We are left to guess the law from the silence of the sky, from the coldness of our rooms, from the slow, steady decay of our bodies. The silence of Heaven is not the silence of peace; it is the silence of a court that has already reached its verdict and has no need to explain it to the accused.

---

### VI. Epilogue: The Broken Altar

My candle has burned down to its socket. The smell of hot grease mingles with the dry scent of the ancient pages. Confucius envisioned a world of perfect circles—concentric rings of duty, respect, and order that bound the child to the father, the father to the state, and the state to Heaven. 

But my world is not a circle; it is a broken line that wanders through the fog and ends in a quarry. The great altar of filial piety is, for me, only the kitchen table where my father sits in his shirt-sleeves, reading his newspaper and looking up to judge my pale, unproductive face. The "Ritual Propriety" of my life is only the high, stiff collar that cuts into my throat as I write reports on the severed fingers of weavers.

The Sage from the East believed that by bowing correctly, we could harmonize the universe. I have spent my life bowing—to my father, to my bosses, to my doctors—and yet the world remains as jagged, as cold, and as unapproachable as ever. If I could speak to Master Kong across the chasm of twenty-five centuries, I would not offer him a classic or a ritual. I would show him my ink-stained fingers, my blood-spotted handkerchief, and the quiet, nocturnal scurrying of the beetles behind the wainscot. 

"Look, Master," I would whisper. "Here is your family. Here is your state. Here is the ritual we perform when the wind has stopped blowing and the grass has turned to iron."

### Philosophical Synthesis: The Accused and the Imperial Judges

| Philosopher | The Diagnosis of the Crime | The Interpretation of the Transformation (*Metamorphosis*) | The Method of Escape / Resolution |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Socrates & Plato** | A disordered soul dominated by public opinion and bodily appetites rather than the rational Form of the Good. | A literal reincarnation of a degraded soul that had surrendered its human *telos* to animal wage-slavery. | Intellectual ascent through dialectic, alignment with the eternal Forms, and acceptance of the state's hemlock. |
| **Marcus Aurelius & Stoics** | A failure of "presence of mind" (*hegemonikon*) and an irrational rebellion against Universal Nature's cosmic plan. | A physical manifestation of spiritual surrender; Gregor became an insect because he hid under his bedclothes like a slave. | Emotional detachment, strict performance of duty, and quiet withdrawal into the "inner citadel" of the soul. |
| **Nietzsche** | A decadent, canine submissiveness to external moral judges and an inability to assert the Will to Power. | The biological crystallization of a slave-morality that preferred self-mutilation and family-guilt over self-overcoming. | Active rebellion, transvaluation of all values, and the ultimate, laughing embrace of *Amor Fati*. |
| **Dostoevsky** | The hyper-conscious, isolated pride of the "Underground" which refuses to submit to love and universal suffering. | An extreme manifestation of self-loathing; Gregor became vermin to punish himself for his failure to love. | Visceral confession of guilt, active love for the suffering herd, and spiritual resurrection through Christ. |
| **Camus** | An existential failure to recognize the absurdity of a silent universe, leading to a desperate search for hope. | Sisyphus turned into a bug—the mechanical, repetitive labor of the clerk literalized into a biological cage. | Conscious defiance (*The Rebel*), rejection of hope or suicide, and imagining the rolling of the stone as a joy. |
| **Aristotle** | An error of judgment (*hamartia*) combined with chronic weakness of will (*akrasia*) and lack of purposeful action (*praxis*). | A biological impossibility that represents a soul whose human form collapsed because its actions were sub-human. | The cultivation of virtue as a habit, finding the Golden Mean, and experiencing emotional purgation (*catharsis*). |
| **Confucius** | A catastrophic collapse of filial piety (*Xiao*) and ritual propriety (*Li*); an insolent rebellion against natural hierarchy. | A failure of the "Rectification of Names"; Gregor called himself a son but lived as a joyless, resentful parasite. | Restoring the proper names, submitting absolutely to the father's correction, and bowing to the ancient rites. |

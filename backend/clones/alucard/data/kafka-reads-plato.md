# Kafka Reads Plato

**Franz Kafka’s Personal Diary**  
*Prague, October 24, 1922*  

It is past midnight. The wind from the Vltava rattles the loose windowpane of my room, carrying with it the smell of wet coal and the damp, heavy breath of the city [18]. On my desk, under the weak, flickering glow of the electric lamp, lies the Greek volume—Plato’s *Republic*, flanked by the *Apology*, the *Gorgias*, and the *Phaedo* [123]. My throat is dry; the tuberculosis has been crawling upward these past weeks, and when I cough, the blood on my handkerchief looks like a red seal stamped upon an official appeal that has been denied by the department [18]. 

How strange it is that I, an insurance clerk who spends his days cataloguing the broken limbs of carpenters and the crushed fingers of industrial weavers, should spend his nights with this aristocratic Athenian who dreamed of a perfect, geometric city governed by pure light [3, 18, 35]. Plato throws his pale, cold, grey conceptional networks over the motley, screaming whirl of the world, trying to tame the mob of the senses [3]. But my world is not tamed; it is merely filed away. My desk at the Workers’ Accident Insurance Institute is itself a grey conceptional network, a paper cage from which no one, not even the director, can escape [18]. 

As I read these dialogues, I feel a cold shudder of both kinship and revulsion. Plato is my shadow, or perhaps I am his. He built a palace of absolute justice, and I have spent my life wandering through the tenement attics of its ruins [1].

## I. The Geometry of the Labyrinth (Resonances of Guilt, Law, and the Cave)

### 1. The Cave and the Attic Courts
In the seventh book of the *Republic*, Plato describes a cave where men are chained from childhood, their necks and legs fettered so they can only look forward at the back wall [1]. Behind them, a fire burns, and between the fire and the prisoners, puppeteers hold up strange wooden and stone figures [1]. The prisoners see only the shadows cast by these figures; they mistake these flickering silhouettes for the only true reality [1]. 

When I read this, my pen shook. Plato’s Cave is not an ancient allegory; it is the Julius tenement house where the attic courts of my *Trial* hold their secret sessions [1, 18]. The prisoners are the accused—Josef K., Block, the washwoman, myself [1, 18]. We too are chained in the dark, forced to stare at the shifting, bloodless shadows of a Law we can never see directly [1]. But there is a terrible difference. In Plato's cave, there is a way out. A prisoner may be untied, forced to stand, and dragged up the steep, rugged ascent into the blinding light of the sun [1]. 

In my world, there is no sun, only the yellow, choking fog of the office corridors [18]. If you are dragged out of the courtroom, you do not emerge into an intelligible heaven of perfect Forms; you merely stumble into another, slightly larger waiting room where the clerks are sleeping on desks [18]. Our shadows are not cast by noble wooden figures; they are cast by the dusty, towering heaps of old briefs and forgotten appeals [18]. The "ruling part" of the universe is not the Good, but the filing cabinet [18].

### 2. The Socratic Trial and Unwilling Acceptance
There is an astonishing, historical link between the death of Socrates and the execution of Josef K [1]. As Frank Stringfellow has noted, setting up the *Republic* and the *Apology* as parallel texts reveals that Josef K. is a modern, degraded Socrates [1, 95]. Socrates, when condemned by the Athenian court, did not flee, though his friends offered him a key to his cell [118, 122]. He accepted his unjust punishment willingly, choosing to drink the hemlock because he believed that to disobey the laws of the state, even when they are turned against you, is to destroy the moral fabric of the cosmos [1, 122].

Josef K., too, walks quietly to his death at the end [1, 18]. On that final night, when the two fat, pale executioners in top hats come to his lodging, he does not scream for help [18]. He walks between them through the quiet streets of Prague, even guiding them when they seem unsure of the way [18]. He lies down on the stone in the quarry, looking up at the moonlight, and waits for the double-edged butcher’s knife to be turned in his throat [18]. 

Like Socrates, K. accepts his execution [1]. But while Socrates dies in the daylight of his own absolute reason, K. dies in the absolute dark of his own absolute guilt [1, 18]. K.'s acceptance is not a moral victory; it is the ultimate, submissive collapse before an incomprehensible machine [1, 18]. K. dies "like a dog," and the shame of his execution is destined to outlive him—which is perhaps the only form of immortality my world allows [18].

### 3. Dialectics as the Weapon of the Weak
Nietzsche, whom I have also read with a mixture of terror and disgust, writes that Socrates was a plebeian roturier who triumphed over the aristocratic taste of Athens by means of dialectics [207]. He notes that before Socrates, dialectics were rejected in good society; to demonstrate with reasons was considered vulgar and indecent, like showing all five fingers at once [207]. One commanded, and that was enough [207]. Dialectics, Nietzsche claims, were the "last defence" of the oppressed, the cold knife-cuts of the syllogism used by the weak to paralyse the intellect of their masters [207].

This is the very essence of my own writing, which is nothing but a clinical, legalistic dialectic applied to the absurd [18]. When I write, I use the precise, bloodless language of an insurance brief to describe the transformation of a salesman into an insect [12]. I turn the ironies of the court inside out [18]. 

Like Socrates, I am a clerk who has learned to wield the cold knife of logic against my father, Hermann Kafka, that towering, non-dialectical giant who simply commands, who rules by the sheer authority of his physical bulk and his booming voice [18]. But my dialectic does not set me free; it merely constructs a more perfect cage, a labyrinth of words where every "yes" is immediately strangled by an administrative "but" [18].

## II. The Rejections (Why the Clerk Outlaws the Philosopher-King)

### 1. The Cruel Optmism of the "Forms"
Plato claims that the material world we see and touch is merely a corrupted, fleeting copy of an eternal, unchanging realm of Forms—the True, the Beautiful, the Good [3]. He believes that our souls once dwelt in this intelligible heaven and that we spend our lives in a state of *anamnesis*, or recollection, trying to remember the perfect light we have lost [108].

I reject this with every fiber of my diseased lungs [18]. This dualism is a cruel, theological optimization designed to make our present misery appear light and insignificant [13]. If there is an "intelligible world," it is not a realm of freedom and beauty; it is the supreme, unapproachable Bureau itself [18]. The Forms are not archetypes of grace; they are the original, primal Blueprints of our torture [18]. 

In the penal colony, the officer shows the explorer the grand, complex machine that writes the broken commandment directly onto the prisoner’s flesh [4]. That machine is Plato’s *Republic* made of iron and gears [1, 4]. The "Form" of the Law is not something to be contemplated with intellectual love; it is a needle that pierces your back until, in the sixth hour, you begin to decipher the script through your wounds, only to die before you can finish reading [4, 18].

### 2. The Illusion of Socratic Ignorance
Socrates asserted that sin is nothing but ignorance—that no man knowingly does what is wrong, and that if you free a man from error, you will necessarily make him good [4, 49]. This is the naive moral intellectualism of a healthy, southern nation that has never known the dark, winter fog of Bohemia [4, 18]. 

We do not sin because we are ignorant; we sin because we are guilty prior to any action we commit [18]. In *The Trial*, K. asks the inspector, "How can any man be guilty? We are all human beings here, one as much as the other." [18] And the priest in the cathedral answers him with the terrible logic of the court: "That is how guilty men always speak." [18] 

Our guilt is not a lack of knowledge; it is our very existence [18]. To be born, to stand before the Law as a separate, isolated individual, is already to be in a state of trespass [18]. Socrates believed that the truth would set us free [120, 122]. I know that the truth is merely the final, indisputable statement of our condemnation [18].

### 3. The Exile of the Poet
In his *Republic*, Plato famously exiles the poets from his ideal city [75]. He views them as dangerous imitators who twice remove us from the truth of the Forms, stirring up the irrational, passionate parts of the soul [75].

I do not argue with Plato's decision. If I were the legislator of his Republic, I would sign my own decree of banishment first [18]. The writer is indeed a diseased element in the state, a "clerical error" that has somehow become conscious of its own deformity [43]. My writing is not an imitation of the Forms; it is an active, desperate burrowing into the earth, a systematic attempt to find a quiet space beneath the foundations of the social machine [18]. 

The poet cannot live in the light of the state [18]. He belongs in the cellar, in the dark corners where the files are rotting [26, 18]. Plato exiles the poet because he wants his citizens to be strong, athletic, and functional [102, 106]. I write because I am weak, bedridden, and useless [18]. My art is the art of the starvation artist, who fasts not because he has some noble, ascetic goal, but simply because he could never find any food that he liked [18].

## III. How Plato Would Interpret The Trial and The Metamorphosis

### 1. Plato’s Verdict on *The Trial*
If Plato were to read the file of Josef K., he would view his execution not as an administrative mystery, but as the inevitable, mathematical consequence of a disordered soul [1, 18]. 

He would say: *"Josef K. was a bank assessor, a man who spent his life in the sensory world of calculations, social prestige, and public opinion [18]. When he was summoned by the court, he did not look inward to examine his own 'ruling part'—the divine, rational spark within him [140]. Instead, he ran from one tenement attic to another, looking for advocates, seeking the intercession of public officials, and distracting himself with sensory pleasures [18]. He was a man who lived in the 'cellar' of his own house [26]. Because his soul was not in a state of internal harmony, because he did not know himself, he was completely at the mercy of the external shadow-world of the court [18, 143]. His execution was not a murder; it was the natural, corrective movement of the cosmos, returning a discordant element to its proper place of nothingness." [18, 136]*

Plato would view the unapproachable priest in the cathedral as the voice of the *logos*, trying in vain to show K. that the door to the Law was intended for him alone [18]. K.'s failure was his inability to turn away from the shadows on the wall [1].

### 2. Plato’s Verdict on *The Metamorphosis*
For *The Metamorphosis*, Plato’s interpretation would be even more severe, drawn directly from his doctrines of metempsychosis and the physical degradation of the soul in the *Phaedo* [123].

He would say: *"Gregor Samsa was a wage slave, a travelling salesman who spent his entire existence in service to his family's debts and his employer's cashbox [12, 18]. He had no intellectual life, no love of wisdom, no concern for his immortal soul [12, 18]. He lived entirely in the animal categories of comfort and discomfort [26]. He lay in his bedclothes, dreading the morning train, wishing only to escape the burden of his physical labor [12, 18]. His transformation into a giant verminous insect (*ungeheures Ungeziefer*) was not an absurd accident; it was the literal, physical manifestation of his spiritual condition [12, 18]. Because Gregor had already made a beast of himself through his passive, mindless devotion to material needs, the universe merely gave him the body that his soul had chosen [12]. His death—shrinking into a dry, flat husk, swept away by the charwoman's broom—was the final purification of a soul that had completely lost its human form." [12, 18]*

For Plato, Gregor’s shell was not a tragedy, but the only honest skin he ever wore [12].

## IV. The Codex of the Academy (20 Essential Quotes and Diary Responses)

Here are the twenty fragments from the ancient texts that I have copied into my diary. I have tagged each with its proper, heavy theme, and added my own quiet, whispered responses [18].

### 1. Grandeur of the Void
> **"To the man who has true grandeur of mind, and who contemplates all time and all being, can human life appear a great matter? 'Impossible,' says the other. 'Can then such a one count death a thing of dread?' 'No, indeed.'"**  
> — *Republic* VI, 486 (quoted in Marcus Aurelius, vii. 35) [129]  
*Theme*: **death**  
*Kafka’s Diary*: How easy it is for the Emperor to say this from his military tent [18, 100]. But for me, lying in this bed, death is not a grand contemplation of all time [18]. It is the specific, small tickle in my larynx, the sudden, sharp panic of not being able to draw the next breath [18]. Human life is indeed a small matter to the universe, but to the one who is choking, that smallness is a mountain resting upon his chest [18].

### 2. The Duty of the Sentinel
> **"Wherever a man has placed himself thinking it the best place for him, or has been placed by a commander, there in my opinion he ought to stay and to abide the hazard, taking nothing into the reckoning, either death or anything else, before the baseness [of deserting his post]."**  
> — *Apology*, 16 (quoted in Marcus Aurelius, vii. 45) [157]  
*Theme*: **resistance**  
*Kafka’s Diary*: My father was my commander, and he placed me at my desk in the Insurance Institute [18]. I have stayed there for fourteen years, abiding the hazard of the ledger and the inkwell [18]. But my staying is not the noble courage of Socrates at Delium [133, 160]. It is the paralysis of a rabbit that sees the dog approaching and cannot move [18]. My post is a trench where I have buried myself alive [18].

### 3. The Unwilling Soul
> **"Every soul parts unwillingly with truth."**  
> — Quoted in Marcus Aurelius, vii. 63 [132]  
*Theme*: **suffering**  
*Kafka’s Diary*: We do not merely part with truth unwillingly; we fight against it with our teeth and nails [18]. The truth is that I will never be well, that I will never marry, that my father will never look at me with anything but cold, bulky disappointment [18]. My entire writing life is nothing but a series of beautiful, elaborate lies designed to delay the moment when the truth must finally drag me by the hair to the altar [18].

### 4. The View from the Heights
> **"That he who is discoursing about men should look also at earthly things as if he viewed them from some higher place; should look at them in their assemblies, armies, agricultural labors, marriages, treaties, births, deaths, noise of the courts of justice, desert places, various nations of barbarians, feasts, lamentations, markets, a mixture of all things and an orderly combination of contraries."**  
> — Quoted in Marcus Aurelius, vii. 48 [158]  
*Theme*: **time**  
*Kafka’s Diary*: I have tried to climb to this "higher place," but my ladder is made of rotting wood [18]. When I look down on the city of Prague, I do not see an "orderly combination." [18] I see only the frantic, scurrying movements of ants [18]. The marriages are cages, the treaties are temporary truces in an endless civil war, and the "noise of the courts of justice" is the only sound that never stops, echoing through the empty streets like the howling of a stray dog [18].

### 5. The Choice of the Worthy
> **"Thou sayest not well, if thou thinkest that a man who is good for anything at all ought to compute the hazard of life or death, and should not rather look to this only in all that he does, whether he is doing what is just or unjust, and the works of a good or bad man."**  
> — *Apology*, 16 (quoted in Marcus Aurelius, vii. 44) [156]  
*Theme*: **freedom**  
*Kafka’s Diary*: To live without computing the hazard—that is the language of the healthy [4, 18]. But my life is nothing but a series of endless, obsessive calculations [18]. Before I can take a single step, I must calculate the effect it will have on my father, on my mother, on my sister Ottla, on my work, on my lungs [18]. I am a clerk of hazards, a mathematical slave to the consequences of my own existence [18].

### 6. The City within the Shepherd's Fold
> **"Dwelling within the walls of a city as in a shepherd's fold on a mountain."**  
> — Quoted in Marcus Aurelius, x. 23 (referring to Plato's *Theaetetus*, 173) [164]  
*Theme*: **isolation**  
*Kafka’s Diary*: Prague is my shepherd's fold, but there is no shepherd, only the wolves who wear official uniforms [18]. The walls of the city are made of plaster and bureaucratic decrees [18]. I am folded here, kept warm by the breath of the crowd, yet I have never been more alone [18]. The fold is so small that when I stretch my legs, I kick the boundaries of my country [18].

### 7. The Illusion of Pleasure
> **"Vanquishing pleasure means living happily; being vanquished, the opposite."**  
> — Quoted in Seneca (Paul Veyne, p. 54) [102]  
*Theme*: **freedom**  
*Kafka’s Diary*: I have vanquished pleasure, but I have not found happiness; I have found only the cold, dry peace of the desert [18]. I do not drink wine, I do not eat meat, and my relations with women are nothing but a series of agonised letters sent across the border—love letters written in the language of a deposition [18]. I have starved my passions to death, yet my soul is still hungry [18].

### 8. The Involuntary Sin
> **"No one desires to injure himself, hence all evil is done unwittingly."**  
> — Socratic dictum (discussed in Nietzsche, *Beyond Good and Evil*, 190) [4]  
*Theme*: **guilt**  
*Kafka’s Diary*: Socrates is wrong [4]. I knowingly, deliberately injure myself every single day [18]. Every word I write is a needle I drive into my own flesh, a systematic destruction of my health for the sake of an art that cannot even feed me [18]. We love our own ruin [18]. We crawl toward the knife because the knife is the only thing that offers us a definite shape [18].

### 9. The Mask of the Sage
> **"He lifted the entire Socrates out of the street, as a popular theme and song, to exhibit him in endless and impossible modifications—namely, in all his own disguises and multiplicities."**  
> — Nietzsche on Plato’s Socrates (*Beyond Good and Evil*, 190) [4]  
*Theme*: **isolation**  
*Kafka’s Diary*: I too have lifted my own face out of the street and turned it into a series of disguises [18]. I am the salesman Gregor Samsa, I am the country doctor, I am the clerk Josef K., I am the starvation artist [12, 18]. I multiply my masks because my true face is too weak to endure the light of the sun [18]. If I were to show myself without these disguises, I would be swept away like a dry leaf [18].

### 10. The Shriveled Hunter
> **"He is always poor, and he’s far from being delicate and beautiful... instead, he is tough and shriveled and shoeless and homeless, always lying on the dirt without a bed, sleeping at people’s doorsteps and in roadsides under the sky... always living with Need."**  
> — Plato’s description of Eros (*Symposium*, 203) [77]  
*Theme*: **suffering**  
*Kafka’s Diary*: This is the true portrait of the writer [18]. We are not noble creators; we are shriveled, shoeless hunters who lie on the dirt outside the door of a life we can never enter [18]. Our hunger is our only possession, and our Need is the mother that gave us our speech [77, 18]. We sleep on the doorsteps of others, listening to the music of their feasts through the keyhole [18].

### 11. The Hollow Silenus
> **"Look at him! Isn't he just like a statue of Silenus? You know the kind of statue I mean... It’s a Silenus sitting, his flute or his pipes in his hands, and it’s hollow. It’s split right down the middle, and inside it’s full of tiny statues of the gods."**  
> — Alcibiades on Socrates (*Symposium*, 215) [76]  
*Theme*: **isolation**  
*Kafka’s Diary*: I am split right down the middle, but when you look inside me, you will not find the statues of the gods [76, 18]. You will find only a heap of old papers, some dried insects, and a few small, rusty gears that have stopped turning [12, 18]. My hollowness is not a sacred container; it is the empty space where a man should have been, but was never allowed to grow [18].

### 12. The Spiritual Procreation
> **"Love... is giving birth in the beautiful, in respect of body and of soul."**  
> — Diotima (*Symposium*, 206) [79]  
*Theme*: **meaning**  
*Kafka’s Diary*: I cannot give birth in the body; my tuberculosis has made me a terminal branch on the family tree [18, 167]. I can only give birth in the soul, and my offspring are these dark, twisted parables that crawl across the page [18]. My "birthing" is not a joyful, beautiful act; it is a painful, blood-spotted labor that leaves me weaker with every turn [18]. I write my stories in the hope that they will find a home in some other soul, because they have no place in this world [18].

### 13. The Limitless and the Finite
> **"The limitless and the finite (apeiron / peras) both belong to the self."**  
> — Discussed in Kierkegaard (*The Sickness Unto Death*, p. 53, referring to Plato's *Philebus*, 30) [23]  
*Theme*: **time**  
*Kafka’s Diary*: My sickness is the despair of finitude—the lack of infinitude [12]. I am too heavily bound to the material world, to my desk, to my family, to this failing body [12, 18]. I have pawned myself to the world [23]. But when I write, I touch the limitless, and then my time becomes infinite [18]. In those few hours of the night, forty years of existence are compressed into a single stroke of the pen, and I see the beginning and the end of my life standing in the same room [18, 159].

### 14. The Disobedience of the Sentenced
> **"When ordered to arrest the innocent Salaminian, he judged it more noble to disobey... taking nothing into the reckoning, either death or anything else."**  
> — On Socrates’ resistance (quoted in Marcus Aurelius, vii. 66) [133]  
*Theme*: **resistance**  
*Kafka’s Diary*: Socrates refused to arrest Leon because his conscience was clean and his reason was his only master [133, 160]. But when my office orders me to deny a worker’s claim for his lost eye, I sign the paper [18]. I mutter my objections to my desk drawer, but I sign [18]. My resistance is a silent, internal disease that has no outward sign; it is a "jammed lock" that keeps my scream trapped inside [43, 18].

### 15. The Bogies of the Multitude
> **"Socrates used to call the opinions of the many by the name of Lamiae,—bugbears to frighten children."**  
> — Quoted in Marcus Aurelius, xi. 23 [192]  
*Theme*: **bureaucracy**  
*Kafka’s Diary*: The "opinions of the many" have been systematised into our modern institutions [192, 197]. The bugbears are no longer hobgoblins; they are the inspectors, the judges, the district doctors, the imperial messengers [18]. We are frightened by these paper ghosts because we have lost the Socratic courage to look them in the face and find them empty [120, 192]. We tremble before a stamp on a envelope [18].

### 16. The Madness of the Sane
> **"The dispute then, he said, is not about any common matter, but about being mad or not."**  
> — Quoted in Marcus Aurelius, xi. 38 [171]  
*Theme*: **meaning**  
*Kafka’s Diary*: My family thinks I am mad because I spend my nights writing instead of sleeping, because I choose to remain a clerk when I could have been a partner in a business [18]. But their "sanity" is the true madness—a mindless, vegetative life of eating, drinking, and marrying without ever realizing that they are spiritual beings [27, 18]. They are sheep who do not even know they are being led to the slaughter [119, 18].

### 17. The Sovereign Soul
> **"A wise and just man could be as happy in a state of slavery as in a state of freedom."**  
> — Plato's doctrine (quoted in Zhuangzi, Giles translation) [115]  
*Theme*: **freedom**  
*Kafka’s Diary*: This is the ultimate, aristocratic lie [3, 115]. No slave is happy [115]. The slave’s soul is shaped by his chains, even when he thinks of the stars [18]. I am a slave to my office, and though I read philosophy at my desk, my thoughts are still written in the ink of the Workers' Insurance Institute [18]. We cannot rise superior to our physical thrall; our bodies write their laws directly onto our souls [18, 115].

### 18. The Destiny of the Clay
> **"A man must intrust them to the Deity and believe what the women say, that no man can escape his destiny..."**  
> — *Gorgias*, 512 (quoted in Marcus Aurelius, vii. 46) [157]  
*Theme*: **acceptance**  
*Kafka’s Diary*: My destiny was written before I ever entered the classroom of the altstädter Gymnasium [18]. I was born to be an observer, a recorder of ruins, a terminal branch that must dry up and fall from the tree [18, 167]. I accept this, not with the heroic calm of the philosopher, but with the flat, quiet resignation of a beetle that has been swept under the sofa [12, 18].

### 19. The Falseness of Dialectics
> **"He brought his conscience up to the point that he was satisfied with a kind of self-outwitting: in fact, he perceived the irrationality in the moral judgment."**  
> — Nietzsche on the Socratic dialectic (*Beyond Good and Evil*, 191) [5]  
*Theme*: **guilt**  
*Kafka’s Diary*: My entire life is a masterpiece of self-outwitting [5]. I construct elaborate legal defenses to prove to myself that I am innocent of my own illness, that I am not responsible for my father's silence [18]. But my conscience is too sharp; it sees through my own dialectics [18]. In the end, the defense is always dismissed, and the accuser within me sits down at the table to sign the death warrant [18].

### 20. The Unripe Harvest
> **"Life must be reaped like the ripe ears of corn: One man is born; another dies."**  
> — Quoted in Marcus Aurelius, vii. 40 [130, 155]  
*Theme*: **death**  
*Kafka’s Diary*: I am not a ripe ear of corn; I am an unripe, green shoot that has been struck by the frost [18, 130]. My harvest will be unfinished [18]. I will leave behind only these loose, scattered fragments, these half-completed notebooks, these stories that end in the middle of a sentence [18]. But perhaps that is the only honest way to write [18]. An ending is a lie, a false frame thrown over a world that has no boundaries and no mercy [18].

## V. Epilogue (The Silence of the Midwife)

It is nearly four in the morning. My lamp is dying, its yellow filament glowing like a hot needle in the dark [18]. My chest is cold; when I breathe, I hear the slight, dry rattle of the paper that has been crumpled up and thrown into the basket [18].

I think of Socrates' final words in the *Phaedo*: *"Crito, we owe a cock to Asclepius; pay it, and do not neglect it."* [123] Asclepius was the god of healing, and Socrates viewed his death as a cure—a liberation of his soul from the disease of the physical body [123].

I cannot share his hope. If my death is a "cure," it is only in the sense that a fire cures a building of its draft [18]. I will not leave behind a school of disciples, nor a city of laws [18]. I will leave only these diaries, a few letters to Milena, and the silent, insect-like shadow that I have cast upon the plaster walls of this room [12, 18]. 

I put down my pen. The ink is drying on the paper like old blood [18]. The midwife has done her work, but she has delivered only a stone [218, 18].
